## 4. Camera rules

Relative scale stays fixed whatever the angle. Measure the bodies. Do not measure the drama.

| Angle | Camera and horizon | How he reads beside a typical human |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Camera near his knee, looking up. The horizon sits low, across the human's chest or his thigh. | He fills the frame. The human is still 5 ft 9 in. Their eyes still meet his upper chest if both are standing. The low tilt does not shrink the human further. |
| Eye level | Camera at the human's eyes, which is his upper chest. The horizon runs through that line. | He is about 1.22 times the human. Neither figure is stretched to make the point. |
| High | Camera above the crest, looking down. The horizon sits high in the frame. | He occupies less of the frame. His head is closer to the lens, so it wants to look too big. Keep the head 1/8.5 of his own height. The human beside him stays 5 ft 9 in. |

Lens, as a full-frame equivalent:

| Lens | Use | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Wide, about 24–35 mm | Establishing shots. More room, more arch, more floor. | Foreground boots look large. After the perspective, he is still 8.5 heads, and the human is still 5 ft 9 in. |
| Normal, about 50 mm | Two-shots and dialogue. | Least distortion. This is the check lens for the upper-chest line. |
| Tele, about 85–135 mm | Visor close-ups. | Depth compresses. A human standing in the same plane does not become a different height because the lens is long. |

Review fails a figure who is the right costume and the wrong size for the room, the doorway, or the person beside them.
